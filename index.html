Research

I study security as a human-system problem: how people understand threats, form trust, make decisions, and recover across digital and AI-mediated environments. Security is often evaluated through the strength of technical protections, yet many of its most consequential failures emerge at the point where those protections meet human behavior. People make security decisions while navigating uncertainty, incomplete information, competing responsibilities, emotional pressure, and systems whose underlying logic is often invisible to them. My research is concerned with what happens at that boundary.

A central direction of my work is understanding the psychology of security. I am interested not only in whether a security product is used, but in whether people can understand what it is doing, recognise when something has gone wrong, know what recovery is available to them, and regain a sense of control afterward. Concepts such as trust, perceived risk, vulnerability, responsibility, agency, and recovery therefore become part of the security problem itself. This perspective is especially important in situations where the lived circumstances do not match the assumptions embedded within a system’s design, or where technically correct security mechanisms create new forms of uncertainty or friction.

My broader research agenda draws from usable security, human-computer interaction, psychology, and human-centered AI to investigate these gaps between system models and lived experience. I am particularly interested in security problems that unfold over time: how trust is established, how people adapt when circumstances change, how access and responsibility shift between people, and what happens after conventional security mechanisms fail or no longer fit the situation for which they were designed.

Ultimately, I want to contribute toward security systems that treat human behavior not as an external source of error, but as a fundamental part of the system being secured. This means moving beyond asking only whether a mechanism is technically secure toward asking whether people can meaningfully understand, navigate, and recover within it—and how those insights can inform the design of more resilient security and AI systems.

About

Hello, I’m Madhulika

I also go by Madhu / Maddie.

I’m someone who has always been curious about people such as how we think, why we make the choices we do, what we notice, what we ignore, and how the things around us quietly shape our behavior.

That curiosity has taken me through cognitive science, design, computer science, security, and research, but it also reaches much further than work. I can spend an unreasonable amount of time thinking about why people make the decisions they do, the logic behind a system, or why something that technically works still feels wrong — and I am unfortunately also just as likely to get absorbed in questions that have no obvious practical answer at all.

A little about me

My path has never felt completely linear. I studied cognitive science because I wanted to understand people: perception, behavior, decision-making, and the strange ways our minds interpret the world around us. Over time, I became increasingly interested in what happens when those human behaviors meet technology.

That eventually brought me deeper into computing, human-computer interaction, and security. Now I spend way too much of my time thinking about systems: the assumptions they make, the people they are designed for, and what happens when real life refuses to behave as neatly as the system expects.

But I don’t think of myself as belonging entirely to one discipline. I enjoy moving between them. I like being able to look at the same problem as a researcher, a designer, a technical person, and simply as a human being trying to understand why something feels confusing, frustrating, unsafe, or unexpectedly meaningful. Perhaps that is also why I tend to collect interests rather than replace them.

I like understanding things. I like making things. And I especially like wondering whether there is another way to look at something entirely.

Things that make me — me!

⌘I like making things

I have always been drawn to making things, although “making” has taken many forms over the years. Sometimes it means building a website or designing an interface. Sometimes it means reorganizing an idea until it finally makes sense on paper. Sometimes it is experimenting with a recipe, imagining a piece of jewelry, painting, decorating a space, or turning a vague idea into something I can actually see and interact with.

I care much too deeply about detail. Not because everything needs to be perfect, but because small details change how something feels. I love the moment when something becomes both useful and beautiful.

✧Stories, mythology & other worlds

I have a soft spot for fantasy, mythology, symbolism, beautiful maps, old architecture, and fictional worlds that feel as though they existed long before you discovered them. I am especially fascinated by mythology because it sits somewhere between imagination, history, belief, psychology, and humanity’s need to make sense of things we cannot fully explain.

I love stories with history in them—places, objects, or characters that seem to carry a life beyond what is immediately visible. Maybe that is why I am so drawn to dark academia, vintage design, intricate objects, and things that feel a little timeless.

I love cooking and baking, especially when I am improvising. I rarely think of recipes as rigid instructions. I am much more likely to start with an idea, change half of it based on what is in the kitchen, taste it repeatedly, and keep adjusting until it feels right.

⌖I like going somewhere I haven’t been before

I love travel, road trips, unfamiliar landscapes, wandering through neighborhoods, finding small shops, seeing architecture in person, and noticing how different places develop their own rhythms.

I am also, admittedly, someone who enjoys planning the trip almost as much as taking it. I like figuring out the route, finding the strange little stops along the way, and creating enough structure that there is still room to wander. For me, the best trips usually have both.

  • You don’t have to fit neatly inside one discipline. Some questions are technical. Some are psychological. Some are philosophical. Some are design problems. And many of the questions I find most interesting become several of those things at once. I like living in that overlap.

Featured project

Adaptive Task Redistribution System

Reducing household fairness issues with equal work does not mean equal burden.

A system that dynamically redistributes household tasks based on each person’s current capacity, context and long-term balance. Rather than a fixed checklist, it adapts to real life — when someone is busy, tired or overloaded, the system rebalances tasks fairly, so everyone contributes and no one carries a disproportionate load over time.

  • Field Behavioural Design
  • Focus Fairness & Equity
  • Method User Research
  • Duration 8 weeks

Status: Concept developed · Research and validation planned

Download PDF

01 The central idea

Fairness should be measured by burden over time—not by whether everyone completes the same number of tasks.

The system takes into account the effort, time and mental load behind each task, and redistributes responsibilities so that the overall burden is balanced fairly across everyone in the household.

02 The problem

Equal does not always mean fair.

Traditional task tracking treats all tasks as equal, but household work varies in time, effort, mental load and frequency. This often leads to imbalanced contributions, frustration and a feeling that one person is always doing more, even if the number of tasks looks similar on paper.

Factors that affect burden

  • Time required Some tasks take longer than others.
  • Mental load Planning and decision-making also take effort.
  • Frequency Recurring tasks add up.
  • Context Energy, schedule and other responsibilities change over time.

Common challenges

  1. Different task effort levels are treated as equal.
  2. One person may take on more invisible or mental work.
  3. Workloads can become unbalanced over time.
  4. Life circumstances change, but task lists do not.
  5. This often leads to resentment and reduced motivation.

03 What the system pays attention to

A more complete picture of work.

The system considers multiple factors to better understand the real effort behind each task.

  • Time and effort

    How long a task takes and how physically demanding it is.

  • Mental load

    Planning, coordination and decision-making required before, during and after a task.

  • Frequency

    How often the task needs to be done (daily, weekly, monthly) and how it accumulates over time.

  • Individual context

    Each person’s current schedule, energy level and other responsibilities outside the home.

  • Fairness over time

    Looking at overall contribution across weeks and months, not just a single day.

04 From task list to a fairer balance

From a task list to a fairer balance.

The system analyses tasks, weights them based on multiple factors, and suggests a fair distribution that adapts over time.

  1. 01

    Input tasks

    Add household tasks or import from templates.

  2. 02

    Assess and weigh

    Analyse time, effort, mental load and frequency for each task.

  3. 03

    Find the right balance

    Compare current workloads and identify imbalances.

  4. 04

    Redistribute

    Suggest a fair task allocation based on current capacity and long-term balance.

  5. 05

    Adapt over time

    Adjust automatically when circumstances or availability change.

05 How it might work in practice

A simple, flexible experience.

The system is designed to fit into real life, with minimal setup and clear, actionable suggestions.

Set up your household

  • Add household members
  • List your recurring tasks
  • Set personal preferences
  • Import from templates

Let the system analyse

  • Tasks are weighted automatically
  • Current availability is considered
  • Imbalances are identified
  • Suggestions are generated

View and adjust

  • See a clear, visual task plan
  • Swap or adjust tasks if needed
  • Provide feedback to improve
  • Keep control of final decisions

Keep it updated

  • Tasks adapt as your schedule changes
  • Rebalance when someone is busy
  • Track fairness over time
  • Build healthier, more sustainable habits

06 Why this is not just a chore app

A fairness system, not just a to-do list.

Most chore apps focus on tracking and reminders. This project goes further by considering the real effort behind each task, adapting to changing circumstances, and prioritising long-term fairness. It supports healthier relationships and a more sustainable way of sharing household work.

The goal is not just to get tasks done.
But to create a fairer and more balanced home for everyone.

07 Next steps before the system is a product

From concept to real-world impact.

The current work is a concept, and several steps are needed to turn it into a product.

  • User testing

    Validate the concept with real households and gather feedback.

  • Explore integrations

    Consider calendar, smart home and existing task tools.

  • Long-term evaluation

    Study the impact on fairness, wellbeing and relationship satisfaction.

  • Refine the model

    Improve task weighting and redistribution logic.

  • Product development

    Develop a full product with a simple and thoughtful user experience.

Featured project

Security Foundations for an Early-Stage Platform

Real product experience → security strategy

A retrospective security case study exploring how a growing platform can build practical security foundations before scale makes those choices harder to reverse.

  • Field Product Security
  • Focus Trust Boundaries
  • Method Retrospective Analysis
  • Context Early-Stage Platform

Status: Completed

Download PDF

01 The central idea

Security is easiest to build when it is treated as part of the product architecture.

The useful output was not a longer list of risks. It was something product and engineering could act on: permission boundaries, requirements, priorities, and a point at which a sensitive feature is safe enough to ship. Some of those controls become much harder to add once the product has already formed around unsafe assumptions.

The platform details, risks, and role models here are generalized for portfolio use. This is a retrospective security strategy exercise, not a claim that these controls were implemented in the original product.

02 The problem

The product was growing. Its security assumptions were growing with it.

I had previously worked on an early-stage platform involving accounts, booking-style workflows, payments, and increasing operational complexity. Later, after building more formal cybersecurity knowledge, I revisited that product through a different lens. Features had become identity boundaries, privileged actions, sensitive data, and external dependencies.

Before evaluating risk, I reduced the product to the parts that changed the security story: identity, payments, data, operations, recovery, and third parties. The goal was not to draw infrastructure. It was to make the trust boundaries understandable.

Questions that changed the story

  • Identity Who is interacting with the system?
  • Access What are they allowed to do?
  • Integrity Can critical actions be trusted?
  • Recovery What happens when assumptions fail?

Where harm could show up

  1. Account takeover, weak recovery, and credential abuse.
  2. Permissions broader than the role, or checks that never reach the server.
  3. Payment misuse, refund abuse, and financial overrides.
  4. Personal data exposed, over-collected, or kept without a clear reason.
  5. Incidents the team cannot see, own, or explain afterward.

03 From features to assets

What would create meaningful harm if it failed?

Security became easier to prioritize once the question shifted from what the product had to what needed to remain confidential, trustworthy, available, or tightly controlled.

  • User accounts

    Account compromise can expose user actions, data, and sensitive workflows.

  • Booking system

    Manipulated or unavailable reservations affect both users and operations.

  • Payment flow

    Incorrect or abused financial actions create immediate trust and business impact.

  • Operational tools

    Internal actions can affect many users, records, or transactions at once.

  • User information

    Unauthorized disclosure creates privacy and reputational risk.

  • Third-party services

    Platform security partly depends on services outside the team’s direct control.

04 From product work to security strategy

A different lens on work that already existed.

The case study is a synthesis. Product experience supplied the workflows. Cybersecurity learning supplied the questions. The strategy is what those two produced together.

  1. 01

    Real product experience

    Requirements, workflows, planning, design coordination, and MVP tradeoffs.

  2. 02

    Cybersecurity learning

    Assets, access control, risk, incident response, logging, and the security lifecycle.

  3. 03

    Security analysis

    Features reframed as trust relationships, permissions, and failure modes.

  4. 04

    Product strategy

    Risk turned into requirements, priorities, launch gates, and product decisions.

05 A question before a sensitive feature ships

Is it safe enough to ship?

A lightweight gate for features that touch identity, sensitive data, payments, or privileged actions. If access boundaries, logging, recovery, and ownership are not in place, the feature goes back. Response later only works if the product leaves enough evidence to understand what happened.

Identity

  • Know who can reach the feature
  • Verify identity before sensitive actions
  • Treat recovery as part of identity
  • Keep an auditable record of recovery

Authorization

  • Separate ordinary and privileged actions
  • Define roles in the product model
  • Check sensitive actions on the server
  • Avoid one broad account for everything

Data

  • Name the information the feature touches
  • Decide who should be able to see it
  • Limit internal access
  • Be explicit about retention

Recovery

  • Plan for a failed workflow
  • Plan for a contested account
  • Make privileged actions reversible
  • Leave enough evidence to explain what happened

06 Authorization is a product decision

Who you are is not the same as what you may do.

Authentication answers who is signing in. Authorization answers what that person should be able to do. As the platform adds operational roles, a single broad account model becomes risky. User, partner, operations, and admin distinctions have to exist in the product model, not only as different screens.

Security risk was not only whether someone could attack the system.
It was also whether the product could make unsafe actions too broad, too easy, or too invisible.

07 Realistic for an early-stage team

Foundations first. Maturity as exposure grows.

The goal was not enterprise security on day one. Controls were separated by when they become expensive to postpone.

  • Now

    Authentication, server-side authorization, payment integrity, recovery safeguards, audit logs, and clear ownership of privileged actions.

  • Next

    Finer roles, monitoring, abuse controls, structured incident handling, and a security review for sensitive features.

  • Later

    Anomaly detection, deeper fraud modeling, and more formal governance once scale and exposure justify them.

08 From risk to a requirement

Something the team can build.

Each example moves from a risk, to what it means for the product, to a requirement someone can implement.

  • Permission boundaries

    Broad account access can allow sensitive operational actions. Roles belong in the product model, and sensitive actions should be checked on the server against those roles.

  • Account recovery

    A weak recovery path can undo an otherwise strong login. Recovery should use verified ownership signals and leave an auditable record.

  • Financial actions

    Refunds, overrides, and payment failures have immediate trust and business consequences. Privileged financial actions should be role-restricted, logged, and recoverable through a known process.

09 Final thoughts

Security that stays attached to how the product works.

The work produced a compact foundation model: a system and trust map, an asset inventory, a threat-surface analysis, a lightweight role and permission model, risk translated into requirements, and a launch gate for high-impact features. Concepts were informed in part by Google Cybersecurity Professional Certificate coursework, then adapted into this product-focused retrospective rather than reproduced as certification material.

Security decisions are strongest when they protect people without becoming detached from the product. The foundations are easiest to build while they can still shape the architecture, not after the product has already scaled around the wrong assumptions.

Second Thought

Designing a more intentional relationship with AI

Second Thought is an evolving browser-extension concept for people who want more control over how AI participates in their thinking. Instead of treating every task as a request for an instant answer, the interface asks a quieter question first: how much help do you want right now?

01 The central idea

The question was not whether AI could help. It was whether every interaction should begin with the same kind of help.

Generative AI interfaces are exceptionally good at removing effort. That is useful—but it can also flatten very different goals into the same interaction pattern: prompt, answer, accept, continue. Second Thought began as an exploration of what happens when the user can define the relationship before the answer arrives.

This is currently a design concept and early prototype direction. The interface is used to reason about the interaction model; it should not be read as a claim that the browser extension has been fully implemented, tested, or launched.

  1. 01

    Notice the default

    Most interfaces are optimized to answer quickly, even when the user may be learning, reflecting, or still forming the problem.

  2. 02

    Surface intention

    Make the user’s goal explicit before the system decides what kind of assistance to provide.

  3. 03

    Set boundaries

    Allow people to control depth, directness, and the kinds of intervention they are comfortable with.

  4. 04

    Revisit the relationship

    Treat AI behavior as adjustable over time rather than a fixed personality or global default.

02 The problem

A system can be helpful and still participate too much.

The design problem is less about restricting capability and more about exposing control. The same person may want Socratic questions while learning, a thinking partner while brainstorming, and direct completion when doing repetitive production work.

Default interaction

  • User prompt A task, question, or half-formed idea.
  • AI answers The system chooses the form and depth of help.
  • User adapts The response becomes the structure of the interaction.

Second Thought interaction

  1. Set intent: what kind of thinking is this session for?
  2. Choose support: how direct, reflective, or collaborative should AI be?
  3. Work together within visible boundaries.
  4. Adjust the relationship as the task changes.

The design question became: can the interface preserve a little more room for the user’s own thinking without making AI frustrating to use?

03 Design principles

The concept is built around agency, not restraint for restraint’s sake.

The interface should not assume that faster completion is always the user’s primary goal. Sometimes the useful behavior is a question, a pause, or a partial structure.

  • Preserve room for thought

    Do not assume that faster completion is always the goal. A question, a pause, or a partial structure can be the more useful behavior.

  • Let users define the relationship

    The person should be able to say whether they are learning, collaborating, or delegating—and adjust that relationship as the task changes.

  • Make intervention visible

    When the system nudges, reframes, structures, or withholds a direct answer, that behavior should come from an understandable setting rather than an invisible rule.

04 Session model

Before the first response, the user sets the tone of the session.

The first design move is intentionally small: ask how the user wants AI to participate. Instead of a large settings page, Second Thought is imagined as a lightweight session layer that can be changed without leaving the workflow. Choose the relationship first. Fine-tune the behavior second.

  • Learn

    Ask questions, reveal structure gradually, and avoid jumping immediately to finished answers.

  • Collaborate

    Explore ideas together, surface alternatives, and build on the user’s reasoning without taking full ownership.

  • Assist

    Be more direct when the user is comfortable delegating the task or wants efficient completion.

Response depth

  • Guided structure when the user wants scaffolding
  • A balanced middle for mixed work
  • More open-ended or direct answers when asked
  • A session-level control, not a hidden default

Optional supports

  • Reflection prompts that keep reasoning visible
  • Presentation suggestions without a full rewrite
  • Explicit toggles rather than hidden assumptions
  • Easy to change without leaving the work

05 Interaction modes

Three modes create a clearer starting point without forcing one personality on every task.

The modes are not meant to be rigid personas. They are working models for different levels of AI participation, with room to refine them through user testing.

Learn

  • Prefer questions before conclusions
  • Explain why, not only what
  • Reveal hints progressively
  • Encourage the user to articulate their own model
  • Avoid producing polished final work too early

Collaborate

  • Offer alternatives and tradeoffs
  • Build on partial ideas
  • Challenge assumptions when useful
  • Keep the user’s framing visible
  • Use reflection without turning the session into a lesson

Assist

  • Be more direct and production-oriented
  • Structure or rewrite when asked
  • Optimize for task completion
  • Keep user-defined boundaries visible
  • Allow a quick switch back to a more reflective mode

The most useful control may not be “how smart should the AI be?” but “what kind of participation do I want from it for this task?”

06 When should the system intervene?

The hardest part is not choosing a mode. It is deciding when the system should act on it.

A reflective system can become just as annoying as an over-eager assistant if it interrupts constantly. The intervention model therefore needs a clear threshold for when to nudge, when to ask, and when to stay quiet.

  • Task intent

    Is the user learning, exploring, drafting, deciding, or simply trying to finish something?

  • Chosen mode

    The system should respect the relationship the user set instead of reverting to a global default.

  • Interruption cost

    A useful reflection prompt in one context may feel intrusive in another. Timing is part of the design.

  • User override

    Any intervention should be easy to dismiss, bypass, or temporarily disable without fighting the system.

07 Current direction

The interface is being used to refine the model before the extension becomes the focus.

The current value of the project is in the interaction logic: what the controls mean, how they work together, and which behaviors are genuinely useful. Implementation comes after the relationship between user intent and AI behavior is clearer.

The prototype is useful for exploring the interaction model, but the behavior is not yet strong enough to treat as a finished product.

08 What exists, and what still needs work

A working visual direction, not a finished product.

  • What exists now

    A side-panel direction, early Learn / Collaborate / Assist modes, response-depth control, optional reflection and presentation supports, and a collapsible-panel concept.

  • Intervention

    When the system should intervene automatically, and how to distinguish learning support from unnecessary friction.

  • Controls

    Whether response depth should be a slider, discrete choices, or contextual controls, and how mid-session mode changes should behave.

  • Trust

    How users understand and trust the extension’s effect on AI behavior.

09 Next steps

The next phase is about testing whether “intentional AI” feels empowering—or simply like more settings.

  1. 01

    Refine onboarding

    Reduce the setup to the fewest controls necessary to communicate the user’s intent clearly.

  2. 02

    Prototype intervention

    Test when reflective nudges help, when they interrupt, and when the system should remain quiet.

  3. 03

    Study mental models

    Explore how people expect an extension like this to affect AI behavior and where confusion appears.

  4. 04

    Evaluate impact

    Examine whether the concept changes perceived agency, trust, learning, or over-reliance during AI-supported work.
